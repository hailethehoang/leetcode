# Do you think people learn more from failure than from success?

## Keep it strict: Point → Support 1 → Support 2 → STOP. Aim for 45–60 seconds.

A possible position is: “Generally, yes. I think failure can be a more powerful learning experience because it forces people to identify what went wrong and change their approach.”

Support 1 — Feedback: Failure exposes weaknesses that success can hide → you have to analyze what went wrong → you can improve a specific skill or decision next time.

Support 2 — Resilience: Experiencing setbacks teaches people how to deal with disappointment and uncertainty → they become more adaptable when they face similar difficulties later.

Useful vocabulary: setback, identify weaknesses, learn from mistakes, reflect on, adjust your approach, resilience, deal with disappointment, constructive feedback.

## Today’s challenge: avoid vague claims

Instead of:

“Failure gives us more experience and makes us better.”

## Build the mechanism:

“When people fail, they are often forced to examine the decisions that led to the result. That reflection helps them identify specific weaknesses and adjust their approach the next time they face a similar problem.”

That pattern is valuable for IELTS Part 3:

Claim → Why? → What happens? → Result.

Your turn: Point → Support 1 → Support 2 → STOP.


# TOPIC 2: Do you think young people today are too focused on achieving success quickly?

Keep it strict: Point → Support 1 → Support 2 → optional Support 3 → STOP. Target 45–60 seconds.

A strong position could be: “Yes, to some extent. I think many young people expect success to come relatively quickly, partly because they are constantly exposed to stories of people becoming successful at a very young age.”

Support 1 — Social media: People mainly see the outcome of someone’s success rather than the years of work behind it → exceptional cases can start to look normal → this creates unrealistic expectations about how quickly a career or business should progress.

Support 2 — Short-term thinking: When results do not come quickly, people may switch jobs, projects, or goals too early → they do not stay with one difficult skill long enough to develop genuine expertise.

Optional counterpoint: Wanting faster progress is not necessarily negative because it can also make people more ambitious and willing to experiment.

Useful vocabulary: instant gratification, unrealistic expectations, overnight success, long-term commitment, delayed gratification, develop expertise, career progression, stick with something

Today’s challenge: distinguish an example from an argument. Don’t say:

“For example, people see successful entrepreneurs on social media.”

Develop the causal chain:

“Social media tends to show the visible result of success while hiding the years of work behind it. As a result, young people may see exceptional success stories as normal and develop unrealistic expectations about their own progress.”

Your rule: Position in the first sentence → exactly 2 developed arguments → STOP.


# Topic 3: Some people think AI will make people less capable of thinking for themselves. To what extent do you agree?

Keep it strict: Point → Support 1 → Support 2 → optional Support 3 → STOP. Target 45–60 seconds.

A strong position could be: “I partly agree. AI can weaken independent thinking if people rely on it for answers without evaluating them, but it can also improve the way we think when it is used as a tool rather than a substitute.”

Support 1 — Overdependence: AI can provide an immediate solution → people may skip the process of analyzing the problem themselves → over time, some problem-solving skills may weaken.

Support 2 — Augmentation: AI can also challenge our assumptions, provide alternative perspectives, and handle routine work → people can spend more time on judgment and higher-level decisions.

Optional Support 3 — Responsibility: The real issue is therefore not AI itself, but how people use it.

Useful vocabulary: independent thinking, critical thinking, over-reliance, evaluate information, challenge assumptions, exercise judgment, cognitive skills, augment human capability, substitute for

Today’s challenge: make a nuanced argument without losing structure. Avoid jumping repeatedly between “AI is good” and “AI is bad.” Finish the negative argument first, then move to the positive one:

Point → Risk → Mechanism → Consequence → Benefit → Mechanism → Consequence → STOP.

One sentence worth practicing:

“The danger arises when people use AI as a substitute for thinking rather than as a tool to support their own judgment.”

Keep the whole answer under 60 seconds. Two well-developed ideas are enough.
